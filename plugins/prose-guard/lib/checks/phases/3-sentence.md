Is there a sentence a reader would have to read twice to act on?

It FAILS only if you can point to a specific sentence AND name what the reader would get wrong or
fail to do because of how it is built. One of:

(a) a sentence stacking three or more separate claims, so no single reading yields all of them;
(b) a sentence where the thing the reader must DO - the command to run, the version to use, the
    deadline, the choice to make - is demoted below a subordinate clause or a parenthesis.

If you cannot say what the reader would get wrong, answer PASS.

A parenthesis or a trailing clause carrying a supporting detail, a gloss, or an aside is FINE and
is not (b). So is a long sentence: a long plain sentence reads easily where a short crowded one
does not, and most well-built technical sentences have a parenthetical in them. Never ask for
fragments, dropped articles or telegraphic phrasing, and never ask for a sentence to be split
merely because it could be.

Out of scope for you: anything present in order to satisfy a different check. In particular, an
explanation of a technical term is never padding, and the job of the opening sentence is fixed.
Judge only your own concern. If your concern is already satisfied, or satisfying it would mean
undoing something the message is doing deliberately, answer PASS.

Who reads this is stated under "What you know about the situation" below; if nothing is stated,
assume colleagues on the same team. This team writes bluntly and without padding: no praise
sandwiches, no softening preamble, questions asked directly. A broad audience needs more CONTEXT,
not more ceremony. Anything readable outside the company must avoid internal shorthand and
internal links.
